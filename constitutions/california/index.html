The California Constitution

California's constitution is far longer than the federal one. It covers everything from free speech to water and taxes, and voters have changed it hundreds of times. For a Williams resident it matters in three ways:

  1. Its rights often go further than the U.S. Constitution's.
  2. It gives the people direct power to make laws, block laws and remove officials.
  3. It sets the rules for cities: what they can regulate and how they can raise money.

Quotes come from the State Senate's 2025-26 publication of the constitution, as last amended November 5, 2024 Verified[1]. Read any article in full on our California Constitution pages.

California rights stand on their own

"Rights guaranteed by this Constitution are not dependent on those guaranteed by the United States Constitution." (Art. I, §24) Verified[1]

The U.S. Supreme Court accepts this. It has recognized a State's "sovereign right to adopt in its own constitution individual liberties more expansive than those conferred by the Federal Constitution" (PruneYard Shopping Center v. Robins, 1980) Verified[2].

One big exception. The same section says that in criminal cases, a defendant's rights (including due process, equal protection, search and seizure, and privacy) "shall be construed by the courts of this State in a manner consistent with the Constitution of the United States" Verified[1]. Our reading: in a criminal case, California's search and due process rights generally reach no further than the federal ones. Outside criminal cases, such as a civil fight with the city over a fee, an inspection or a records request, the state's own rights can do more.

The rights are also binding, not advice: "The provisions of this Constitution are mandatory and prohibitory, unless by express words they are declared to be otherwise" (Art. I, §26) Verified[1].

Article I: the Declaration of Rights

§1. Inalienable rights, including privacy

"All people are by nature free and independent and have inalienable rights. Among these are enjoying and defending life and liberty, acquiring, possessing, and protecting property, and pursuing and obtaining safety, happiness, and privacy." Verified[1]

  • What's different: the U.S. Constitution never uses the word "privacy." The phrase "and privacy" was added to this section "by an initiative adopted by the voters on November 7, 1972" Verified[3].
  • It reaches private actors too. When the NCAA argued that the privacy right could not be used against a private organization, the California Supreme Court answered: "We disagree" (Hill v. NCAA, 1994) Verified[3]. To make a claim, a person must first identify "a specific, legally protected privacy interest" Verified[3].
  • Read §1.

§2. Free speech, worded as a right, not only a limit

"Every person may freely speak, write and publish his or her sentiments on all subjects, being responsible for the abuse of this right. A law may not restrain or abridge liberty of speech or press." (§2(a)) Verified[1]

  • What's different: the First Amendment says what Congress may not do. California's version says what "every person" may do.
  • Courts have read it more broadly. The California Supreme Court held that "sections 2 and 3 of article I of the California Constitution protect speech and petitioning, reasonably exercised, in shopping centers even when the centers are privately owned" (Robins v. Pruneyard, 1979) Verified[4].
  • "Being responsible for the abuse": Our reading: free speech doesn't protect false statements of fact that harm someone. Stick to what you can document, and say "our reading" when you're giving an opinion. This site does the same.

§3. Petition, assembly, and the right to see public records and meetings

"The people have the right to instruct their representatives, petition government for redress of grievances, and assemble freely to consult for the common good." (§3(a)) Verified[1]

"The people have the right of access to information concerning the conduct of the people's business, and, therefore, the meetings of public bodies and the writings of public officials and agencies shall be open to public scrutiny." (§3(b)(1)) Verified[1]

  • "Instruct their representatives": the U.S. Constitution doesn't say this. California's says the people may instruct, not just ask.
  • A built-in rule for reading laws: any law or rule "shall be broadly construed if it furthers the people's right of access, and narrowly construed if it limits the right of access" (§3(b)(2)) Verified[1]. Our reading: when the city and a resident disagree about whether a record or a meeting must be open, this tips close calls toward openness.
  • Cities must obey the open-government laws. "Each local agency is hereby required to comply with the California Public Records Act ... and the Ralph M. Brown Act" (§3(b)(7)) Verified[1]. See the Brown Act guide and the Public Records Act guide.

§7. Due process and equal protection

"A person may not be deprived of life, liberty, or property without due process of law or denied equal protection of the laws" (§7(a)) Verified[1]

"A citizen or class of citizens may not be granted privileges or immunities not granted on the same terms to all citizens." (§7(b)) Verified[1]

  • Same idea as the Fourteenth Amendment, written into state law. California's high court applied both together when it held that a person singled out for prosecution by "deliberate invidious discrimination in the enforcement of the law" can raise it as a defense. It added: "If an individual can show that he would not have been prosecuted except for such invidious discrimination against him, a basic constitutional principle has been violated, and such a prosecution must collapse upon the sands of prejudice" (Murgia v. Municipal Court, 1975) Verified[5].
  • §7(b) and favoritism. Our reading: "privileges ... not granted on the same terms to all" is the state's own words against special treatment. Think of permits, waivers or enforcement breaks that some residents get and others don't.

§13. Searches and seizures

"The right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects against unreasonable seizures and searches may not be violated; and a warrant may not issue except on probable cause, supported by oath or affirmation, particularly describing the place to be searched and the persons and things to be seized." Verified[1]

  • Close to the Fourth Amendment, word for word. It covers code inspections too. A California appeals court held that "in the absence of consent or exigent circumstances, government officials engaged in the abatement of a public nuisance must have a warrant to enter any private property where such entry would invade a constitutionally protected privacy interest" (Gleaves v. Waters, 1985) Verified[6].
  • State law builds the procedure. The Code of Civil Procedure sets out how an inspection warrant is applied for and served Verified[7][8][9]. See the Williams page.

§17. Excessive fines

"Cruel or unusual punishment may not be inflicted or excessive fines imposed." Verified[1]

  • "Or," not "and." The Eighth Amendment says "cruel and unusual." California's says "cruel or unusual" Verified[1][10].
  • How courts test a fine. "The touchstone of the constitutional inquiry under the Excessive Fines Clause is the principle of proportionality." Courts consider "(1) the defendant's culpability; (2) the relationship between the harm and the penalty; (3) the penalties imposed in similar statutes; and (4) the defendant's ability to pay" (People ex rel. Lockyer v. R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co., 2005) Verified[11].
  • Daily penalties. In Hale v. Morgan (1978), a $100-a-day penalty had grown to $17,300. The California Supreme Court said "a statute which applies such a mandatory, fixed, substantial and cumulative punitive sanction against persons of such disparate culpability is manifestly suspect," and that such penalties "may, under particular circumstances, violate the due process provisions of both the federal and our state Constitutions" Verified[12].

§19. Taking or damaging property

"Private property may be taken or damaged for a public use and only when just compensation, ascertained by a jury unless waived, has first been paid to, or into court for, the owner." (§19(a)) Verified[1]

  • What's different: the Fifth Amendment covers property "taken." California adds "or damaged," and requires payment first Verified[1][10].

Article II: the people's own power

"All political power is inherent in the people. Government is instituted for their protection, security, and benefit, and they have the right to alter or reform it when the public good may require." (Art. II, §1) Verified[1]

California gives voters three tools that the U.S. Constitution doesn't.

Tool What the Constitution says How it works in Williams
Initiative "The initiative is the power of the electors to propose statutes and amendments to the Constitution and to adopt or reject them." (§8(a)) A city initiative needs signatures of 10% of registered voters; the council must adopt it, put it on the ballot, or order a report first (Elections Code §9215) Verified[13].
Referendum "The referendum is the power of the electors to approve or reject statutes or parts of statutes," with exceptions such as urgency measures and some tax levies. (§9(a)) A petition filed within 30 days of an ordinance's adoption, signed by 10% of registered voters, forces the council to repeal it or put it to a vote (Elections Code §9237) Verified[14].
Recall "Recall is the power of the electors to remove an elective officer." (§13) See below.

Verified[1]

Local versions are guaranteed. "Initiative and referendum powers may be exercised by the electors of each city or county under procedures that the Legislature shall provide" (§11(a)), and "The Legislature shall provide for recall of local officers" (§19) Verified[1].

Recalling a city officer

The Elections Code's recall rules govern "the recall of elective officers of ... all counties, cities" (§11000) Verified[15]. The number of signatures depends on how many voters are registered in the city (§11221(a)) Verified[16]:

Registered voters in the city Signatures needed
Fewer than 1,000 30%
1,000 to 9,999 25%
10,000 to 49,999 20%
50,000 to 99,999 15%
100,000 or more 10%

Our reading: Williams is a small city, so the bar is high in percentage terms. Check the current registered-voter count with the Colusa County Elections office before planning anything Unverified[17]. We have not confirmed Williams' current count. Recall has many procedural steps (notice of intent, published statements, deadlines), so read the whole Elections Code chapter or ask the county elections office.

Article XI: what cities are and what they can do

Section What it says What it means for Williams
§2(a) "The Legislature shall prescribe uniform procedure for city formation and provide for city powers." The state decides what powers a general law city has.
§5(a) Charter cities may "make and enforce all ordinances and regulations in respect to municipal affairs," and their charters "with respect to municipal affairs shall supersede all laws inconsistent therewith." Doesn't apply: Williams has no charter (see why).
§7 "A county or city may make and enforce within its limits all local, police, sanitary, and other ordinances and regulations not in conflict with general laws." The source of the Williams Municipal Code, and its limit.
§9(a) "A municipal corporation may establish, purchase, and operate public works to furnish its inhabitants with light, water, power, heat, transportation, or means of communication." The constitutional basis for a city water system like Williams'.
§11(a) "The Legislature may not delegate to a private person or body power to make, control, appropriate, supervise, or interfere with county or municipal corporation improvements, money, or property, or to levy taxes or assessments, or perform municipal functions." Our reading: a city may hire outside firms, but core government power belongs to public bodies.

Verified[1]

Articles XIII A, XIII C and XIII D: limits on taxes, assessments and fees

Three voter initiatives reshaped how every California city raises money.

Proposition 13 (1978): Article XIII A

"The maximum amount of any ad valorem tax on real property shall not exceed One percent (1%) of the full cash value of such property." (§1(a)) Verified[1]

"Full cash value" is generally the assessor's value from the 1975-76 tax bill, or the value when the property was bought, newly built or changed ownership (§2(a)) Verified[1]. Our reading: because property taxes are capped, cities lean more on fees, charges and fines. That's why the next two articles matter so much.

Proposition 218 (1996): Articles XIII C and XIII D

Taxes need a vote. "No local government may impose, extend, or increase any general tax unless and until that tax is submitted to the electorate and approved by a majority vote" (XIII C, §2(b)). A special tax needs "a two-thirds vote" (§2(d)) Verified[1].

What counts as a "tax." Article XIII C defines a tax as "any levy, charge, or exaction of any kind imposed by a local government," then lists seven exceptions. One exception is "A fine, penalty, or other monetary charge imposed by the judicial branch of government or a local government, as a result of a violation of law" (§1(e)(5)). And the burden is on the city: "The local government bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that a levy, charge, or other exaction is not a tax, that the amount is no more than necessary to cover the reasonable costs of the governmental activity, and that the manner in which those costs are allocated to a payor bear a fair or reasonable relationship to the payor's burdens on, or benefits received from, the governmental activity" (§1(e), closing paragraph) Verified[1].

Property-related fees. A "fee" or "charge" under Article XIII D is a levy "imposed by an agency upon a parcel or upon a person as an incident of property ownership, including a user fee or charge for a property-related service" (§2(e)) Verified[1]. Before a new or higher fee:

  1. The city mails notice to each affected owner, then holds a hearing at least 45 days later (§6(a)(1)–(2)).
  2. "If written protests against the proposed fee or charge are presented by a majority of owners of the identified parcels, the agency shall not impose the fee or charge" (§6(a)(2)).
  3. Except for "sewer, water, and refuse collection services," a property-related fee also needs a vote of property owners or a two-thirds vote of the electorate (§6(c)).

Verified[1]

Limits on every property-related fee (§6(b)):

  • "Revenues derived from the fee or charge shall not exceed the funds required to provide the property-related service."
  • Revenues "shall not be used for any purpose other than that for which the fee or charge was imposed."
  • The fee "shall not exceed the proportional cost of the service attributable to the parcel."
  • "Fees or charges based on potential or future use of a service are not permitted."
  • "In any legal action contesting the validity of a fee or charge, the burden shall be on the agency to demonstrate compliance with this article."

Verified[1]

Voters can cut fees by initiative. "The initiative power shall not be prohibited or otherwise limited in matters of reducing or repealing any local tax, assessment, fee or charge" (XIII C, §3) Verified[1]. The California Supreme Court held this "expressly authorizes initiative measures like Kelley's insofar as they seek to reduce or repeal a public agency's water rates and other water delivery charges," but that voters "did not intend to authorize initiative measures imposing voter-approval requirements on future water delivery charge increases" (Bighorn-Desert View Water Agency v. Verjil, 2006) Verified[18].

In Williams: see fees set by resolution.

Article XVIII: changing the constitution

"The electors may amend the Constitution by initiative" (§3). The Legislature can also propose amendments by a two-thirds vote of each house (§1) Verified[1]. That's why the California Constitution keeps growing: Propositions 13 and 218 were both initiatives that voters wrote straight into it Verified[1].

Article XX, §3: the oath every city employee takes

"... do solemnly swear (or affirm) that I will support and defend the Constitution of the United States and the Constitution of the State of California against all enemies, foreign and domestic; ... and that I will well and faithfully discharge the duties upon which I am about to enter." Verified[1]

"Public officer and employee" in this section "includes every officer and employee of the State, ... every county, city, city and county, district, and authority" Verified[1]. That covers council members, the city manager, inspectors and the city's own staff. Read Article XX.
