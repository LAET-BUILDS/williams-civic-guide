The cases behind the rights

A constitution's words are short. Courts decide what they mean in real disputes, and those decisions bind later cases. Below is every case this section relies on. For each one we read the opinion text ourselves; the link goes to a free copy. A case's holding is what it decided. Anything else in an opinion is explanation.

Read the whole opinion before you rely on a case. A quote out of context can mislead, and later cases can narrow or change a rule. We have not checked every later case that cites these decisions. Links go to free copies (Cornell's Legal Information Institute, CourtListener, Justia, and the California Attorney General). These are not the official reporters.

U.S. Supreme Court

Case What happened What the Court said Why it matters here
Marbury v. Madison (1803) A dispute over an undelivered judicial commission. "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is." Verified[1] Courts, not cities, decide what the Constitution means.
Barron v. Baltimore (1833) A wharf owner said city work ruined his property. The takings clause "is intended solely as a limitation on the exercise of power by the government of the United States, and is not applicable to the legislation of the states." Verified[2] Before the Fourteenth Amendment, the Bill of Rights didn't bind states or cities.
Yick Wo v. Hopkins (1886) San Francisco denied laundry permits to Chinese applicants under a neutral-sounding ordinance. A law "fair on its face" but applied "with an evil eye and an unequal hand" denies equal protection; the Fourteenth Amendment "is not confined to the protection of citizens." Verified[3] Equal protection covers how a city enforces, and covers non-citizens.
Camara v. Municipal Court (1967) A San Francisco tenant was charged with a crime for refusing a warrantless housing inspection. He "had a constitutional right to insist that the inspectors obtain a warrant to search." Verified[4] Code inspectors need consent or a warrant, except in emergencies.
Coates v. City of Cincinnati (1971) A city made it a crime to be "annoying to persons passing by." "Unconstitutionally vague"; "Conduct that annoys some people does not annoy others." Verified[5] Vague city rules can fail.
Grayned v. City of Rockford (1972) A protester challenged two city ordinances. Explained why vague laws fail; upheld the antinoise ordinance as "not invalid on its face." Verified[6] The test for vagueness, and a reminder that clear noise rules can stand.
Mathews v. Eldridge (1976) Disability benefits were cut off before a hearing. Due process means being heard "at a meaningful time and in a meaningful manner," judged by three factors. Verified[7] The yardstick for any city procedure, including deposits to contest.
PruneYard Shopping Center v. Robins (1980) California protected petitioning at a private mall. States have a "sovereign right to adopt in its own constitution individual liberties more expansive than those conferred by the Federal Constitution." Verified[8] California's rights can go further than federal rights.
United States v. Bajakajian (1998) The government sought to take $357,144 for a reporting violation. A punitive forfeiture is unconstitutional "if it is grossly disproportional to the gravity of the offense." Verified[9] The federal test for excessive fines.
Village of Willowbrook v. Olech (2000) A village demanded a bigger easement from one family than from others to connect water. A "class of one" can claim equal protection. Verified[10] One resident treated differently can sue, if there's no rational basis.
McDonald v. Chicago (2010) A challenge to a city handgun ban. "The Fourteenth Amendment incorporates the Second Amendment right." (from the official syllabus) Verified[11] A modern example of incorporation against a city.
Lozman v. City of Riviera Beach (2018) A critic was handcuffed and removed during council public comment. "The existence of probable cause does not bar Lozman's First Amendment retaliation claim under the circumstances of this case." Verified[12] Retaliating against a speaker at a council meeting can be challenged.
Timbs v. Indiana (2019) Indiana took a $42,000 vehicle over a drug sale. "The Excessive Fines Clause is therefore incorporated by the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment." Verified[13] Excessive-fines protection binds states and cities.
Knick v. Township of Scott (2019) A township ordinance required public access to a private cemetery. A property owner "may bring a Fifth Amendment claim under §1983" when property is taken without compensation. Verified[14] Takings claims against local government can go straight to federal court.
Tyler v. Hennepin County (2023) A county sold a condo over a tax debt of about $15,000 and kept the $25,000 surplus. "A taxpayer is entitled to the surplus in excess of the debt owed." Verified[15] Government can't keep more than it's owed in a tax sale.

California courts

Case What happened What the court said Why it matters here
Murgia v. Municipal Court (1975) 15 Cal.3d 286 Farm-worker union members said they were prosecuted because of their union. A prosecution based on invidious discrimination "must collapse upon the sands of prejudice," but simple laxity or "nonarbitrary selective enforcement" is not a violation. Verified[16] How to raise discriminatory enforcement, and how hard it is.
Hale v. Morgan (1978) 22 Cal.3d 388 A $100-a-day civil penalty reached $17,300. A "mandatory, fixed, substantial and cumulative punitive sanction" applied to people of very different culpability is "manifestly suspect." Verified[17] Daily penalties that stack can be challenged.
Robins v. Pruneyard (1979) 23 Cal.3d 899 Students gathered petition signatures at a private mall. Art. I, §§2 and 3 "protect speech and petitioning, reasonably exercised, in shopping centers even when the centers are privately owned." Verified[18] California speech rights reach further than federal ones.
Gleaves v. Waters (1985) 175 Cal.App.3d 413 A dispute over warrantless entry to abate a nuisance. Officials abating a nuisance "must have a warrant to enter any private property where such entry would invade a constitutionally protected privacy interest," absent consent or emergency. Verified[19] The warrant rule applies to nuisance abatement in California.
Sherwin-Williams Co. v. City of Los Angeles (1993) 4 Cal.4th 893 Paint companies said state law preempted a Los Angeles rule on the retail display of aerosol paint. The court held it was not preempted. Local law that "duplicates, contradicts, or enters an area fully occupied by general law" is preempted and "void." Verified[20] The test for when state law beats a city ordinance, and a reminder that cities often win.
Hill v. NCAA (1994) 7 Cal.4th 1 College athletes challenged drug testing by a private association. The privacy right can be enforced against private entities, and the claim starts with "a specific, legally protected privacy interest." Verified[21] California's privacy right is broad.
People ex rel. Lockyer v. R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. (2005) 37 Cal.4th 707 Penalties for handing out free cigarette samples. Four factors for excessive fines, including "the defendant's ability to pay." Verified[22] The California test for excessive fines.
Bighorn-Desert View Water Agency v. Verjil (2006) 39 Cal.4th 205 A resident's initiative to cut water rates and require votes on increases. Voters can reduce water charges by initiative, but can't impose a vote requirement on future increases this way. Verified[23] What Prop 218 lets residents do about water rates.
